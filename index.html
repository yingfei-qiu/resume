Holding the buyer’s intent from search to order placed, so nobody has to tell Shopee the same thing twice.

The challenge

Shopee already owns the shopping habit in Southeast Asia. The next order is won inside the journey, not at acquisition.

When a platform already has the traffic and the repeat behaviour, growth has to come from converting sessions that are already happening. That makes the stretch between a search and a placed order the most valuable surface in the product, and the friction inside it the most expensive kind.

#1
Purchase frequency in Southeast Asia. Habit, not reach, is the moat.
~$13–15
Average order value, roughly 2–3× TikTok Shop’s. Each lost basket is worth more.
~70%
Of carts abandoned across e-commerce. Closer to 80% on mobile.

Sources: Cube SEA platform GMV analysis (2026); Digital in Asia, Shopee vs TikTok Shop (2026); Baymard Institute meta-analysis of 50 studies (70.22%); Dynamic Yield mobile abandonment.

The problem in one sentence

A buyer states their intent once, and the journey then drops, hides or never asks for it, at three separate points.

At search
Intent dropped
“song cho stainless steel pot”

The engine relaxes the query to protect result count, then shows Song Cho aluminium pots beside unbranded steel ones. It never says it did this.

While filtering
Intent hidden

Filters sit behind an icon and scroll away with the header. The constraint the buyer just set is no longer visible while they browse.

At checkout
Intent never asked

Delivery fee, arrival date, address and pick-up options all appear for the first time at the last step, the most expensive place to surprise anyone.

Each loss is small. Together, the buyer does the platform’s work three times over, and every re-statement is another chance to leave.

2 of 5

Research and insights

I started from my own failed searches, then watched other people fail in exactly the same three places.

  1. Self-documented friction

    Logged a week of real failed sessions on Shopee SG, annotated against Nielsen’s ten heuristics. The Song Cho search is verbatim.

    Visibility of system status and user control broke repeatedly.

  2. Unaided observation

    Watched family members who aren’t confident with apps try to order the same pot on their own phones, thinking aloud, with no prompting.

    The same three stalls surfaced unprompted: nobody found the filter icon, nobody trusted the aluminium results, and the delivery fee surprised everyone at checkout.

  3. Heuristic evaluation

    Scored every screen from product page to checkout, rating issues by frequency and impact.

    Three severity-3 (major) issues clustered around fulfilment disclosure.

  4. Desk research

    Tested each hypothesis against published abandonment and choice-overload research, so the case doesn’t rest on one person’s annoyance.

    All three frictions map to a documented, quantified abandonment cause.

  5. Competitor teardown

    Compared how Lazada, TikTok Shop, Amazon and Taobao handle query interpretation, buyer confidence and fulfilment choice.

    No competitor connects what the buyer typed to how the parcel reaches them.

Insight 1: the problem isn’t catalogue size

Search falls back to matching individual words to keep the result count high. That’s a reasonable engineering trade-off. The design failure is that relaxed results share the same grid and styling as exact ones, so the buyer has to re-judge the relevance of every tile.

Choice-overload research is specific about why this hurts. Iyengar and Lepper recorded roughly 30% purchase from a limited set against about 3% from an extensive one, and later work at Northwestern found overload bites hardest when options can’t be easily told apart. Decision fatigue here comes from options the buyer can’t differentiate, not from how many there are.

Insight 2: the costliest surprise arrives last

Why shoppers abandon a checkout (share of abandoners citing each reason)
Unexpected extra costs48%
Checkout too long or complicated22%
Delivery too slow~21%

All three are fulfilment problems. Showing delivery cost on the product page or cart instead of checkout is associated with 17% lower abandonment.

Baymard Institute; Searchlab 2026 compilation.

Buyers don’t object to paying for delivery. They object to finding out at the last step, and to discovering that “pick up nearby” was an option only after committing to a home address. Shopee already runs a dense pick-up and locker network across Singapore, and currently discloses it at the worst possible moment.

Limits of this evidence. This is desk research, self-observation and informal sessions with people I know, not moderated testing with a recruited, representative sample. The study that would validate it is the first step of the implementation plan.

Proposed solution

One principle resolves all three frictions as a single system, rather than three separate patches.

The buyer states their intent once. The platform holds it, shows it, and never asks again.

The chips a buyer sees on the search screen are the same held state that pre-fills the product page, groups the cart and completes checkout. That shared object is what makes this a system, and it’s why shipping the search piece first unlocks the rest.

Implementation

Ship the search intent-lock first. It’s the cheapest to build, it gives the cleanest A/B read, and the other moves depend on the object it creates.

Priorities and sequencing

  1. Week 1
    Five-person moderated test on the search screenThe cheapest possible answer to the riskiest assumption. Task: find a Song Cho stainless steel pot under $60, and watch whether anyone touches a chip unprompted.
  2. Weeks 2–3
    Intent-lock behind a feature flagHigh-specificity queries only: multiple words that include a recognised brand. Small blast radius, clean read.
  3. Weeks 4–6
    Product page match and fulfilment blockExtend only if click distribution across bands holds. These read the parse, so they can’t go first.Gate: exact band out-earns relaxed bands
  4. Quarter 2
    Cart grouping and confirmation checkoutRolled out once the delivery and collection mix model clears the unit-economics guardrail.Gate: cost per order holds under the new mix

Feasibility

The search engine already performs query relaxation; this proposal surfaces a decision the system is making anyway rather than inventing a new one. Brand and material facets exist in listing data for structured sellers, and the pick-up network and collection picker already exist at checkout. The new engineering is the held-intent object and moving the fulfilment estimate earlier.

Risks and trade-offs

Parsing fails on messy queries
Impact HighLikelihood Likely at the edges

The one I’d lose sleep over. A parser that confidently misreads a query is worse than none, because it makes a wrong reading look authoritative. Chips must degrade gracefully: parse what’s confident, leave the rest as free text, never invent a facet.

Buyers ignore the chips
Impact HighLikelihood Unknown

If buyers read chips as decoration rather than controls, the system loses its anchor. Week 1’s moderated test exists to answer this before anything is built.

Relaxed bands cannibalise exact ones
Impact MediumLikelihood Plausible

Instrument click distribution across bands. If band two out-earns band one, the segmentation logic is wrong, not the concept.

Collection shift breaks unit economics
Impact MediumLikelihood Measurable

Model cost per order under different delivery and collection mixes before rollout, rather than discovering it in the P&L.

Success metrics

Four metrics show whether it works. Three guardrails make sure it doesn’t quietly break something else.

Primary metrics

Search to product page click-through Expect up

Measured on the exact-match band specifically. If the bands work, the first band earns a disproportionate share of clicks.

Product page to add to cart Expect up

Tests whether “why this matched” and the fulfilment block actually reduce hesitation.

Cart to order completion Expect up

The headline conversion measure. Isolate sessions that used a collection point to see whether choosing fulfilment early is doing the work.

Query refinements before first product page Expect down

A buyer who retypes their search is a buyer the results failed.

Guardrails

Discovery revenue Must hold

Segmenting results could suppress the adjacent browsing Shopee monetises. Watch GMV from non-exact-match tiles.

Unclaimed parcels Must hold

Pushing collection could shift cost into parcels nobody picks up. Watch the collection rate within the 7-day hold.

Seller fairness Must hold

Bands must not become a hidden ranking penalty for smaller sellers whose listings are less structured.

The challenge

Tutors find assignments in Telegram groups and web portals built as walls of text, with no way to filter by subject, place or time.

For a student tutor, every irrelevant post scrolled past is unpaid time. Three questions decide whether an assignment is worth taking: is it my subject, can I get there, and does it fit my week? Existing channels make the tutor answer all three manually, for every post.

2 of 5

Research and insights

Research surfaced three pain points, and each one maps to one of those three questions.

Is it my subject?
No filters

Third-party channels have no subject or level filters, so tutors scroll through hundreds of irrelevant posts to find their niche.

Can I get there?
Location guesswork

Tutors toggle between the listing and Google Maps to judge each commute, one post at a time.

Does it fit my week?
Timeslot clash

Common time slots are worked out by hand, often after the tutor has already invested in a match.

Persona: Jason Tan

A Year 2 business student in Pasir Ris with a five-module load and heavy CCA commitments. His goal is concrete enough to design against: three to four Primary Math students within 2 km of home, earning $600+ a month, with every lesson falling inside the gaps in his Google Calendar. Mapping his journey showed the same sequence each time: open several channels, meet a wall of text, scroll manually for relevant posts.

3 of 5

Design decisions

The most useful thing testing did was change our minds, twice.

Personality-style matching quiz
High-quality filters the tutor controls

Our first idea replaced filtering with a quiz that matched tutors to students. Heuristic testing showed a black-box match wasn’t the right fit: tutors want autonomy over which jobs they take.

Evidence: heuristic testing
Calendar sync as the final step
Calendar sync as the first filter

Checking availability at the end meant tutors could reach a match and then discover a clash, a late-stage drop-off. Moving sync to discovery means every suggested student is already pre-validated against the tutor’s real availability.

Logic: remove the cause of late drop-off
4 of 5

The experiment

Calendar sync carried the whole availability-first idea, so we didn’t leave its design to taste. We tested it.

Question
Which entry point do tutors most clearly recognise as “sync my calendar”?
Hypothesis
A calendar icon communicates the function more clearly than a text “Sync” button.
Design
Between-subjects: each participant saw only one version, so learning from one variant couldn’t contaminate the other.
Variable
Only the sync control changed. Every other element of the screen was identical.
Tool
Maze, with a separate test link per group
Decision rule
Adopt the variant only if the difference is significant at the 5% level
p = 0.00010378
Well below the 0.05 threshold. Participants were clearer with the calendar icon, so the icon shipped in the final prototype.
5 of 5

Results and reflection

We put the finished prototype in front of tutors and asked them to compare it with the channels they use today.

Share of participating tutors who agreed
Found it easier to identify suitable assignments100%
Preferred it to third-party channels100%
Found the design easy to navigate95%

Moving schedule and location filtering to the start of the journey reduced the decisions tutors had to make per listing, which is where the preference came from.

What I’d do differently with the numbers

The results are encouraging, but reading them critically is part of the job. If I ran this again:

  • Report sample size and effect size next to every p-value. A p-value says the difference is unlikely to be chance; it doesn’t say how big the difference is, which is what a team needs to prioritise.
  • Treat 100% with caution. With a small sample, a confidence interval on a proportion is wide, so “all participants agreed” is a strong signal, not a guarantee at scale.
  • Pair attitudes with behaviour. Self-reported ease is useful, but task success rate and time to first correct click would show whether the design actually performs better.
  • Recruit beyond our network. Testing with tutors outside our circle would reduce the chance that friendly participants inflated the results.

The lesson I took into Shopee: replacing noise with structured data helps people decide, and every design claim deserves a measure decided in advance.
